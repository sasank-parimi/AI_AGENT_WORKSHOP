## Motivation

Intrinsic motivation comes from interest or satisfaction in the activity itself. Extrinsic motivation is driven by a separable outcome such as a reward, grade or recognition.

Self-determination theory proposes that autonomy, competence and relatedness support more self-directed motivation. A manager can support these needs by offering meaningful choice, useful feedback and a sense of connection without removing clear expectations.

Expectancy theory links effort to three judgments: whether effort can improve performance, whether performance will lead to an outcome, and whether that outcome is valued. A reward alone may not motivate if any link is weak.

## Situational leadership

Situational leadership asks a leader to adjust the balance of direction and support to the needs of a particular person and task. Someone new to a task may need clearer direction, while someone experienced may benefit from autonomy and targeted support.

The approach is not a fixed label for a person. Readiness can vary across tasks and over time. A useful application identifies the task, the person’s current capability and confidence, and why a particular balance of direction and support fits.

## Psychological safety

Psychological safety is a shared belief that a team can take interpersonal risks, such as asking questions, admitting mistakes or challenging an idea, without humiliation or punishment.

Psychological safety does not mean avoiding disagreement or lowering standards. Teams can combine psychological safety with accountability by making it safe to surface problems while remaining clear about responsibilities and quality.

Leader behaviours that can support psychological safety include acknowledging uncertainty, inviting dissent, responding constructively to bad news and separating a useful mistake report from blame.
